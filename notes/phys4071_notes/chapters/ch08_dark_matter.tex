\section{Evidence for Dark Matter}
\begin{intuition}
    Dark matter is a form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect light, making it invisible to electromagnetic observations. Its existence is inferred from its gravitational effects on visible matter, radiation, and the large-scale structure of the universe. \\

    There are multiple lines of evidence for dark matter, including: gravitational lensing, expansion rate of the universe, the CMB anisotropies, and other measurements. We will focus on the most direct evidence for dark matter: galaxy rotation curves, in below sections. We will also briefly discuss an alternative explanation for the observed phenomena: Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND).
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{extra}
    The name of dark matter was first named d by Jacobus Kapteyn in 1922, he claimed that "dark matter" was not required in galactic Solar neighbourhood. In 1932, Jan Oort suggested that there was twice as much dark matter as luminous matter in the Solar neighbourhood. However, latter observations showed that he was wrong, therefore the discovery of dark matter was credited to Fritz Zwicky who made the first correct claim for the existence of dark matter in 1933. Zwicky concluded from measurements of the redshifts of galaxies in the Coma cluster that their velocities are much larger than the escape velocity due to the visible mass of the cluster. \\

    Between 1950s and 1970s, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford built upon earlier galactic rotation studies and conducted a systematic survey of spiral galaxies.  While their pioneering 1970 paper on the Andromeda galaxy hinted at anomalous outer velocities, it was their work in the mid-to-late 1970s that definitively demonstrated flat rotation curves across many galaxies. These curves showed that stars and gas orbit at nearly constant speeds far beyond the visible disk, rather than declining as expected from luminous matter alone. This systematic evidence firmly established that galaxies are embedded in massive, extended halos of dark matter.
\end{extra}

\subsection{Galaxy Rotation Curves}
\begin{foundation}
    Start from the Newtonian gravity, the rotational velocity $v$ of a star orbiting at a distance $r$ from the center of a galaxy can be derived from the balance of gravitational force and centripetal force:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{GM(r)}{r^2} &= \frac{v^2}{r} \\
        v^2 &= \frac{GM(r)}{r}
    \end{align*}
    Then assuming that the mass distribution of the galaxy is concentrated in the center, and density decreases with distance:
    \[
        \rho \propto r^{-n}
    \]
    where $n > 0$, but $n < 3$ to ensure that the total mass is finite. For typical values, $n \approx 2 \sim 3$.

    Now we can get the mass distribution of the galaxy:
    \begin{align*}
        M(r) &= \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2 \rho(r') dr' \\
        &\propto \int_0^r r'^{2-n} dr' \\
        &\propto r^{3-n}
    \end{align*}
    Therefore, the rotational velocity can be derived as:
    \begin{align*}
        v^2 &\propto \frac{r^{3-n}}{r}
    \end{align*}
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    In newtonian gravity, the rotational velocity $v$ of a star orbiting at a distance $r$ from the center of a galaxy can be expressed as:
    \[
        v = r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}
    \]
    where $n$ is the power-law index of the mass density distribution of the galaxy.
\end{theory}

\clearpage
%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    For $n \approx 2$, we have $v \propto r^0$, which means that the rotational velocity $v$ is constant at large distances from the galaxy center, which is consistent with the observed flat rotation curves. \\
    
    However, from the visible matter distribution, we expect $n > 2$ as stellar density falls rapidly at edges of galaxies, which would predict a declining rotation curve. The conflict between the expected declining rotation curve and the observed flat rotation curve is one of the key pieces of evidence for the existence of dark matter, which provides additional mass that extends beyond the visible matter, leading to a flat rotation curve.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

    \caption{The rotation curve of a galaxy: The red curves represent the expected rotation curve based on the visible matter distribution, which shows a declining trend at large distances. The white curve represents the observed rotation curve, which remains flat at large distances, indicating the presence of dark matter.}
    \label{fig:flat_rotation_curve}
\end{figure}

\begin{extra}
    To describe the observed rotation curves like above figure, the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile is commonly used to model the dark matter halo of galaxies. The NFW profile is given by:
    \[
        \rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{\frac{r}{r_s} \left( 1 + \frac{r}{r_s} \right)^2}
    \]
    where $\rho_0$ is a characteristic density and $r_s$ is a scale radius. The NFW profile predicts a density in 3 parts of the galaxy: 
    (1) Inner region ($r \ll r_s$): $\rho(r) \propto r^{-1}$, which means that density drops but velocity increases as we slightly move away from the center of the galaxy. It matches the rapid increase of the rotation curve in the inner region of the galaxy. \\
    
    (2) Intermediate region ($r \approx r_s$): $\rho(r) \propto r^{-2}$, which means that the density decreases and the velocity remains constant as we move further away from the center of the galaxy. It matches the flat rotation curve in the intermediate region of the galaxy. \\
    
    (3) Outer region ($r \gg r_s$): $\rho(r) \propto r^{-3}$, which means that the density decreases more rapidly and the velocity decreases as we move further away from the center of the galaxy. It matches the declining rotation curve in the outer region of the galaxy.
\end{extra}

\subsection{Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND)}
\begin{intuition}
    Note that existence of dark matter is just a hypothesis to explain the observed phenomena, and there are alternative explanations. One of the most popular alternative explanation is Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), which was proposed by Mordehai Milgrom in 1983. MOND modifies Newton's second law of motion at very low accelerations, which are typically found in the outer regions of galaxies. According to MOND, the effective gravitational force becomes stronger than predicted by Newtonian gravity at these low accelerations, which can explain the flat rotation curves of galaxies without invoking dark matter.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}
    In MOND, an interpolation function $\mu(a/a_0)$ is introduced too bridge the gap between the Newtonian regime (where $a \gg a_0$) and the MOND regime (where $a \ll a_0$). The modified equation of motion can be written as:
    \begin{align*}
        a \mu \left( \frac{a}{a_0} \right) = a_N = \frac{GM}{r^2}
    \end{align*}
    where $a$ is the actual acceleration, $a_N$ is the Newtonian acceleration, and we required $\mu(x) $ that:
    \begin{align*}
        \mu (x) = \begin{cases}
            1 & \text{for } x \gg 1 \quad \text{(Newtonian regime)} \\
            x & \text{for } x \ll 1 \quad \text{(MOND regime)}
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}

    A common choice for the interpolation function is:
    \begin{align*}
        \mu(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}
    \end{align*}
    Here we only consider the Deep-MOND regime, where $a \ll a_0$, the equation of motion can be simplified to:
    \begin{align*}
        a \mu \left( \frac{a}{a_0} \right) &= a_N \\
        a \left( \frac{a}{a_0} \right) &= a_N \\
        a^2 &= a_0 a_N \\
        a &= \sqrt{a_0 a_N} \\
        \frac{v^2}{r} &= \sqrt{a_0 \frac{GM}{r^2}} \\
        v^4 &= G M a_0
    \end{align*}
    This result shows that the rotational velocity $v$ becomes constant at large distances from the galaxy center, which is consistent with the observed flat rotation curves.
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    MOND suggest an modified theory of Newton's laws, with:
    \[
        F = m a \mu \left( \frac{a}{a_0} \right)
    \]
    where $\mu(x)$ is an interpolation function that transitions between the Newtonian regime and the MOND regime, and $a_0$ is a characteristic acceleration scale below which MOND effects become significant, and by galaxy-scale observations, $a_0$ is found to be approximately $1.2 \times 10^{-10} \, \text{m/s}^2$. And we can see that as $a_0$ is extremely small, our labatory experiments are not sensitive enough to detect the MOND effects. \\

    Meanwhile, if scale up to galaxy scale, where the acceleration is much smaller than $a_0$:
    \[
        v = (G M a_0)^{1/4}
    \]
    the rotational velocity $v$ becomes constant at large distances from the galaxy center, which is consistent with the observed flat rotation curves. \\
\end{theory}

\clearpage
%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    While MOND can successfully explain the flat rotation curves of galaxies, it faces 2 main challenges: \\

    (1) Evidence of DM can be found in different physical systems (e.g. galaxy clusters, CMB anisotropies, large scale structure), and if we want to use MOND to explain all these phenomena, gravity must be modified in a more complex way, while the dark matter hypothesis can explain all these phenomena simply. \\

    (It is more related to the principle of Occam's Razor, which states that among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected, another similar case is the geocentric model and the heliocentric model, where the former can explain the observed phenomena by introducing epicycles, while the latter can explain all the phenomena without introducing any epicycle.) \\

    (2) MOND cannot explain the Bullet Cluster, which is a collision of two galaxy clusters. The gravitational lensing observations of the Bullet Cluster show that most of the mass is located in the regions where there is little visible matter, which is consistent with the dark matter hypothesis but cannot be explained by MOND.
\end{intuition}

Therefore, we will expect to see the distribution of dark matter and visible matter are separated. On the other hand, if MOND is correct, we will expect to see the distribution of dark matter and visible matter are the same, because there is no dark matter in MOND. \\
    
    The observations of the Bullet Cluster show that the distribution of dark matter and visible matter are separated, which is consistent with the dark matter hypothesis but cannot be explained by MOND.
\end{extra}

\subsection{Baryonic and Non-baryonic Dark Matter}
\begin{intuition}
    We can classify dark matter into two categories: baryonic and non-baryonic. Baryonic dark matter consists of ordinary matter (protons, neutrons, electrons) that does not emit or absorb significant light, such as MACHOs (Massive Astrophysical Compact Halo Objects), which include black holes, neutron stars, brown dwarfs, and rogue planets. The primary method to detect MACHOs is gravitational microlensing, which occurs when a MACHO passes in front of a background star and temporarily magnifies its light. However, microlensing surveys have detected too few events to account for the total dark matter in galactic halos, indicating that MACHOs can only make up a small fraction of dark matter. \\

    Non-baryonic dark matter candidates, such as WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles), axions, and sterile neutrinos, are strongly favored by current observations. Cosmological data from the cosmic microwave background and Big Bang nucleosynthesis show that ordinary baryonic matter accounts for only about 5\% of the universe's total mass-energy budget, while dark matter makes up roughly 27\%, proving that the vast majority of dark matter must be non-baryonic. These candidates are also well-motivated theoretically, as they naturally arise in extensions of the Standard Model of particle physics. Unlike baryonic dark matter in the form of compact objects, non-baryonic dark matter does not produce discrete microlensing events that temporarily brighten background stars. Instead, it exerts a smooth, diffuse gravitational influence, which is fully consistent with observed galactic rotation curves, large-scale structure, and cosmological lensing.
\end{intuition}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]

    \caption{Gravitational microlensing: When a MACHO passes in front of a background star, it can temporarily magnify the light from the star, creating a characteristic light curve.

\end{figure}

%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{extra}
    In case you have zero knowledge about star evolution, below is a brief introduction of the formation of some objects that can be MACHOs: \\

    (1) Brown Dwarfs: Stars are formed from the gravitational collapse of molecular clouds, and the mass of the collapsing cloud determines the type of star that is formed. If the mass of the collapsing cloud is less than about 0.08 times the mass of the Sun, it will not be able to sustain nuclear fusion in its core, and it will become a brown dwarf. Brown dwarfs are sub-stellar objects that are too massive to be planets but not massive enough to be stars. They emit mainly in the infrared and are very faint, making them difficult to detect. \\

    (2) White Dwarf: when the star reach its end of life, it will collapse under its own gravity, and if the mass of the collapsing part of star is below the Chadrasekhar limit (about 1.4 times the mass of the Sun), it will become a white dwarf. White dwarfs become fade and cool over time, and finally become black dwarfs, which are cold and dark objects that do not emit any light. \\

    (3) Neutron Star: While if the mass of the collapsing part of star is above the Chadrasekhar limit but below the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (about 2-3 times the mass of the Sun), it will become a neutron star. It is an extremely dense object composed mostly of neutrons, and is super small with only about 10-20 kilometers in diameter. \\

    (4) Black Hole: If the mass of the collapsing part of star is above the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, it will become a black hole. Black holes are regions of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from it. They can be formed from the collapse of massive stars, and they can also be formed from the merger of smaller black holes or from the collapse of dense regions in the early universe.
\end{extra}

\subsection{Cold and Hot Dark Matter}
\begin{intuition}
    Dark matter is classified based on its velocity distribution (or free-streaming length) at the time of decoupling: cold dark matter (CDM), warm dark matter (WDM), and hot dark matter (HDM). HDM consists of particles that were relativistic at decoupling, typically with very small masses (eV scale or less, like active neutrinos). Their high thermal velocities cause significant free-streaming, which erases small-scale density fluctuations. This would lead to a "top-down" structure formation scenario, where the largest structures form first and later fragment, which is inconsistent with the observed hierarchical, "bottom-up" formation of galaxies and clusters. Consequently, HDM can only constitute a minor fraction of the total dark matter budget. Active neutrinos are the primary example of HDM. \\

    On the other hand, CDM consists of particles that were non-relativistic at decoupling. This category spans a wide mass range: it includes heavy thermal relics like WIMPs (GeV–TeV scale) and extremely light non-thermally produced particles like axions ($\mu$eV–meV scale). CDM's low velocity dispersion allows gravitational collapse on small scales first, driving the bottom-up hierarchical structure formation that precisely matches cosmological observations. The total dark matter density is approximately 27\% of the critical density of the universe, with CDM comprising the dominant component. Current cosmological and astrophysical data strongly favor a CDM-dominated universe.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Stableness and Lifetime}
\begin{intuition}
    What makes a particle stable (or sufficiently long-lived)? There are two primary conditions: 
    (1) The particle must carry a conserved quantum number (e.g., electric charge, baryon number, or a discrete symmetry charge like a $Z_2$ parity). 
    (2) It must be the lightest particle carrying that conserved quantity. If a lighter state exists with the same conserved charge, the heavier particle can decay into it while respecting the conservation law. \\

    For example, the electron is absolutely stable because it is the lightest particle with non-zero electric charge. Similarly, the proton is effectively stable because it is the lightest baryon, and baryon number is conserved at the perturbative level in the Standard Model. Conversely, the muon and tau leptons are unstable because they are heavier than the electron and can decay via the weak interaction into lighter leptons while conserving total lepton number. \\
    
    Therefore, if dark matter consists of particles, they must be stable or have lifetimes significantly exceeding the age of the universe ($\tau_{\mathrm{DM}} \gtrsim 10^{17}\,\mathrm{s}$). This typically requires that dark matter particles carry an exactly or approximately conserved quantum number (often imposed via a discrete symmetry) and be the lightest state charged under it, or that their decay channels are highly suppressed by symmetry, large mass scales, or tiny couplings.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{extra}
    The lifetime of other objects as reference:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Electron: > 4.6 \times 10^{26} \text{ years}
        \item Muon: 2.2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ seconds}
        \item Tau: 2.9 \times 10^{-13} \text{ seconds}
        \item Proton: > 10^{34} \text{ years (current experimental lower limit)}
        \item Age of the universe: \approx 1.38 \times 10^{10} \text{ years}
    \end{itemize}
\end{extra}

\subsection{Decoupling and Interactions}
\begin{intuition}
    So far, we have known the properties of dark matter as: \\
    (1) It is non-luminous and electrically neutral. \\
    (2) It is non-baryonic and very likely to be a new type of particle that does not exist in the Standard Model. \\
    (3) It is cold (non-relativistic) at the time of decoupling, (i.e. $m_\chi \gg T_{\mathrm{CMB}}$), which means that it has a very small velocity dispersion and can form structures on small scales. \\
    (4) It is stable or has a lifetime much longer than the age of the universe. \\

    Now lets discuss about the interactions and relic abundance of dark matter. 
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    If we assume the DM particles remain in thermal equilibrium and never decoupled, then the number density of DM particles would be given by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution:
    \[
        n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} = g_\chi \left( \frac{m_\chi T}{2\pi} \right)^{3/2} e^{-m_\chi/T}
    \]
    And we will see that the number density of DM particles would be exponentially suppressed at low temperatures, which means that the relic abundance of DM would be negligible. Therefore, we need to consider the case where DM particles decoupled from the thermal bath at some point in the early universe, which allows them to have a non-negligible relic abundance today. \\

    Generally, assumed the stable DM particle $\chi$ is in thermal equilibrium with the Standard Model particles $Y$ in the early universe, and the dominant interaction is $\chi \bar{\chi} \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$. \\
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    At the early stage of the universe $T \gg m_\chi$, the process of $\chi \bar{\chi}$ creation and annihilation are in equilibrium, leading $\chi$ to be present in large quantities alongside the various Standard Model particles. \\
    
    As the universe expands and cools down, the temperature drops below the mass of the DM particle ($T < m_\chi$), and the process of $\chi \bar{\chi}$ creation becomes exponentially suppressed due to the Boltzmann factor, while the annihilation process continues to deplete the number of $\chi$ particles. \\
    
    Eventually, when the annihilation rate falls below the expansion rate of the universe (i.e., $\Gamma_{\mathrm{ann}} < H$), the DM particles "freeze out" and their comoving number density becomes constant, leading to a relic abundance that can account for the observed dark matter density today.
\end{intuition}

\begin{example}{ Equation of DM interactions}{}
    Why the equation is $\chi \bar{\chi} \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$, but not $\chi \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$? \\

    \bigskip\textbf{Solution.}\quad \\
    If the process is $\chi \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$, then the DM particle $\chi$ can decay into Standard Model particles $Y$ and $\bar{Y}$, which would violate the stability requirement of dark matter. Since we know that dark matter must be stable or have a lifetime much longer than the age of the universe, the process $\chi \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$ is not allowed. \\
    
    Further, if the process is $\chi \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$, the quantum numbers of $\chi$ must be the same as the combined quantum numbers of $Y$ and $\bar{Y}$, which would require $\chi$ to carry 0 value of the quantum numbers. This also violates the requirement that dark matter must carry a conserved quantum number to ensure its stability.
\end{example}

\subsection{Boltzmann Equation for DM Relic Abundance}
\begin{foundation}
    Recall the general form of the Boltzmann equation for the number density $n_\chi$ of DM particles:
    \[
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi = C[n_\chi]
    \]
    Where LHS actually is the number density conversation equation (you can check chapter 7.3.2 to see how the $3H n_\chi$ term arises), and RHS is the collision term that describes the net effect of annihilation of DM particles. For the process $\chi \bar{\chi} \leftrightarrow Y \bar{Y}$, the collision term can be written as:
    \[
        C[n_\chi] = -\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle (n_\chi^2 - n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \]
    where $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is the thermally averaged annihilation cross section times velocity, $n_\chi$ is the actual number density of DM particles, and $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$ is the equilibrium number density of DM particles. The term $n_\chi^2$ represents the annihilation of DM particles, while the term $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2$ represents the creation of DM particles from the thermal bath. \\

    On the other words:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Forward process $\chi \bar{\chi} \to Y \bar{Y}$: the rate is proportional to $n_\chi^2$ because it requires two DM particles to annihilate.
        \item Reverse process $Y \bar{Y} \to \chi \bar{\chi}$: the rate is proportional to $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2$ because it depends on the equilibrium abundance of DM particles that can be produced from the thermal bath.
    \end{itemize}

    Now we define the decay rate as:
    \[
        \Gamma_{\chi} = \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}
    \]
    Substituting the collision term into the Boltzmann equation, we get:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi &= - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2 \left( \frac{n_\chi^2}{n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2} - 1 \right) \\
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi &= n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} \Gamma_\chi \left( 1 - \frac{n_\chi^2}{n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2} \right)
    \end{align*}
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The Boltzmann equation for the number density of DM particles can be written as:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi &= n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} \Gamma_\chi \left( 1 - \frac{n_\chi^2}{n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2} \right) \\
        &= - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle (n_\chi^2 - n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \end{align*}
    where $\Gamma_\chi$ is the decay rate of DM particles, $n_\chi$ is the actual number density of DM particles, and $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$ is the equilibrium number density of DM particles.
\end{theory}
    
\bigskip \noindent
\begin{importantnote}
    Some may confused with the 2 notations $n_\chi$ and $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$, the former is the actual number density of DM particles, which means the real number density of DM particles we are having in the universe at the given time, and it affects the rate of DM annihilation, because the more DM particles we have, the more likely they will annihilate with each other.

    While the latter is the equilibrium number density of DM particles, which means the number density of DM particles we would have if they were in thermal equilibrium with the thermal bath at the given time. It depends only on current temperature T via statistical mechanics: 
    \[
        n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} = g_\chi \left( \frac{m_\chi T}{2\pi} \right)^{3/2} e^{-m_\chi/T}
    \]
    and it affects the rate of DM creation, because it shows the number density of DM particles that can be produced from the thermal bath at the given time.
\end{importantnote}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
     Therefore, at early universe $T \gg m_\chi$, we have $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} \approx n_\chi$, which means rate of DM creation and annihilation are balanced, and DM particles are in thermal equilibrium. \\
    
    As the universe expands and cools down, we have $T < m_\chi$, which means $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} < n_\chi$, the DM particles are no longer in thermal equilibrium, and the annihilation process dominates over the creation process. \\
    
    And at the freeze-out point, we have $\Gamma_\chi = H$, which means the annihilation rate equals the expansion rate of the universe, and $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} \ll n_\chi$, but the expansion of the universe is so fast that the DM particles cannot find each other to annihilate, so the term $n_\chi^2 - n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2$ is cancel out, which freeze out the number density of DM particles.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{remark}
    In real case, the stage of $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} < n_\chi$ is just short as when $m_\chi < T$, the decay rate $\Gamma_\chi \propto n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$ also drops as $e^{-m_\chi/T}$, which means the 2 conditions nearly happen at the same time, and the freeze-out happens immediately after the DM particles are no longer in thermal equilibrium. So we can treat the intermediate stage and freeze-out stage as the same stage.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Comoving Abundance}
\begin{foundation}
    First, define the comoving abundance of DM particles as:
    \[
        Y_\chi \equiv \frac{n_\chi}{s}  = \frac{n_\text{dec} a^{-3}}{s_\text{dec} a^{-3}} = \frac{n_\text{dec}}{s_\text{dec}}
    \]
    The $Y_\chi$ is the number of DM particles per unit entropy, which is a convenient quantity to use because it remains constant after freeze-out, even as the universe continues to expand and cool down.\\

    and we can rewrite the LHS of the Boltzmann equation as:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi &= s \frac{dY_\chi}{dt} + Y_\chi \frac{ds}{dt} + 3H s Y_\chi 
    \end{align*}
    Note the entropy density $s$ is $propto a^{-3}$, which means $\frac{ds}{dt} = -3H s$, so we have:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi &= s \frac{dY_\chi}{dt} + Y_\chi (-3H s) + 3H s Y_\chi 
        = s \frac{dY_\chi}{dt} 
    \end{align*}

    Similarly, we can rewrite the RHS of the Boltzmann equation as:
    \begin{align*}
        - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle (n_\chi^2 - n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2) &= - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle s^2 (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \end{align*}
    Dividing both sides by $s$, we get:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dt} &= - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle s (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2) \\
    \end{align*}

    Now, lets define the inverse temperature variable $x = \frac{m_\chi}{T}$, and:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{m_\chi}{T} \right) \\
        &= - \frac{m_\chi}{T^2} \frac{dT}{dt} \\
        &= - \frac{m_\chi}{T^2} (-H T) \\
        &= H x
    \end{align*}
    which we using the fact that the temperature of the universe scales as $T \propto a^{-1}$, so:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dT}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(C a(t)) \\
        & = \frac{C}{a^2} \frac{da}{dt} \\
        & = - \frac{T a}{a^2} \dot{a} \\
        & = - H T
    \end{align*}

    Therefore, we can rewrite the Boltzmann equation as:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} \frac{dx}{dt} &= - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle s (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2) \\
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx}  &= \frac{1}{H x} \left( - \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle s (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2) \right) \\
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} &= - \frac{\lambda}{x^2} (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \end{align*}
    with $\lambda = \frac{s \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle}{H} x$. \\

    In fact, we can get details value of $\lambda$ by substituting the expressions of $s$ and $H$:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{cases}
        s &= \frac{2\pi^2}{45} g_{*s} T^3 \\
        H &= \sqrt{\frac{8\pi G}{3} \rho} = \sqrt{\frac{8\pi G}{3} \frac{\pi^2}{30} g_* T^4}
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    Note here we assume the decoupling happens during the radiation-dominated era, so the energy density $\rho$ and the entropy density $s$ are dominated by the relativistic particles, and we use the relativistic in chpater 5.2 and 5.4 to get the above expressions. \\

    Defining the Planck mass as $M_{\mathrm{Pl}} = \frac{\hbar c}{\sqrt{G}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18} \, \mathrm{GeV}$, we can rewrite the expression of $\lambda$ as:
    \begin{align*}
        \lambda &= \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \frac{s}{H} x \\
        &= \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \frac{\frac{2\pi^2}{45} g_{*s} T^3}{\sqrt{\frac{8 \pi^3}{90}} \frac{\sqrt{g_*}}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}} T^2} x \\
        &= \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \left( \frac{2\pi^2}{45} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{90}{8 \pi^3}} \right) \cdot \frac{g_{*s}}{\sqrt{g_*}} \cdot M_{\mathrm{Pl}} \cdot T x\\
        &= \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \left( \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}} \right) \cdot \sqrt{g_{*}} \cdot M_{\mathrm{Pl}} \cdot m_\chi
    \end{align*}
    where we take the assumption that $g_{*s} \approx g_*$ in last step, which is valid when there is no significant change in the number of relativistic degrees of freedom around the freeze-out temperature (i.e. before the neutrino decoupling). \\
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The Boltzmann equation for the comoving abundance can be written as:
    \[
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} = - \frac{\lambda}{x^2} (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \]
    with $\lambda = \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \left( \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}} \right) \cdot \sqrt{g_{*}} \cdot M_{\mathrm{Pl}} \cdot m_\chi$.
\end{theory}

\subsection{Freeze-out Temperature}
%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    Through the above Boltzmann equation, we can see that the term $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is the key parameter that determines the relic abundance of DM particles. If $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is too large, the DM particles will annihilate too efficiently during the $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} < n_\chi$ stage, leading to a very small relic abundance. \\

    On the other hand, if $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is too small, the DM particles will not annihilate efficiently, and they will freeze out with a large number density, leading to a relic abundance that exceeds the observed dark matter density. \\

    Now lets find out the freeze-out temperature and the relic abundance of DM particles by solving the Boltzmann equation. We will see that if $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is around the weak scale, we can get the correct relic abundance that matches the observed dark matter density, which is known as the WIMP miracle.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}
    Recall the comoving abundance Boltzmann equation:
    \[
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} = - \frac{\lambda}{x^2} (Y_\chi^2 - Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2)
    \]

    Around freeze-out the DM is non-relativistic ($x\gg 1$), so using the Maxwell--Boltzmann form
    \[
        n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}=g_\chi\left(\frac{m_\chi T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}e^{-m_\chi/T}=g_\chi\left(\frac{m_\chi T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}e^{-x}
    \]
    and the radiation-era entropy density
    \[
        s=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_{*s}T^3\simeq\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_*T^3
    \]
    we get
    \begin{align*}
        Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}} &= \frac{n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{s} \\
        &=\frac{g_\chi\left(\frac{m_\chi T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}e^{-x}}{\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_*T^3} \\
        &=\frac{45}{2\pi^2}\frac{g_\chi}{g_*}\left(\frac{m_\chi}{2\pi T}\right)^{3/2}e^{-x} \\
        &=\frac{45}{2\pi^2}\frac{g_\chi}{g_*}\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}e^{-x}
    \end{align*}
    Its logarithmic derivative is
    \[
        \frac{d\ln Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{dx}=\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{3}{2}\ln x - x\right]=\frac{3}{2x}-1
    \]

    \textbf{Define freeze-out as an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ departure from equilibrium.}\\
    Define the fractional deviation
    \[
        \Delta(x)\equiv\frac{Y_\chi-Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}
        \quad\Rightarrow\quad
        Y_\chi=(1+\Delta)Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}
    \]
    Then the 2 sides of the comoving abundance Boltzmann equation can be written as:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{cases}
            \frac{dY_\chi}{dx}
            &=\frac{d}{dx}\big[(1+\Delta)Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}\big]
            =(1+\Delta)\frac{dY_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{dx}+Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}\frac{d\Delta}{dx}\\
            Y_\chi^2-Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2
            &=\big[(1+\Delta)^2-1\big]Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2
            =\Delta(\Delta+2)\,Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    Substituting into the $dY/dx$ equation gives
    \[
        (1+\Delta)\frac{dY_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{dx}+Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}\frac{d\Delta}{dx}
        =-\frac{s\langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle}{Hx}\,\Delta(\Delta+2)\,Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2
    \]
    We define freeze-out by $\Delta(x_{\mathrm{FO}})=c$ with $c\approx 0.5$ (an empirical ${\cal O}(1)$ constant), and approximate that $\Delta$ varies slowly right at the transition, i.e.
    \(\left.\frac{d\Delta}{dx}\right|_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}\approx 0\). Then at $x=x_{\mathrm{FO}}$:
    \[
        (1+c)\left|\frac{dY_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{dx}\right|
        \simeq \frac{s\langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle}{Hx_{\mathrm{FO}}}\,c(c+2)\,Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2
    \]
    Divide by $Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$ and use
    \(\left|\frac{d\ln Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}}{dx}\right|=1-\frac{3}{2x}\simeq 1\) for $x\gg 1$:
    \[
        Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}(x_{\mathrm{FO}})
        \simeq \frac{Hx_{\mathrm{FO}}}{s\langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle}\,\frac{1+c}{c(c+2)}
        \approx \frac{Hx_{\mathrm{FO}}}{s\langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle}\,\frac{1}{c(c+2)}
    \]
    The $(1+c)$ term is dropped as the pre-factors of order unity(e.g. $c=0.5$) are negligible on the results of $x_{\mathrm{FO}}$. \\

    \textbf{Use radiation-era $H(T)$ and solve for $x_{\mathrm{FO}}$.}\\
    In radiation domination
    \[
        H=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^3g_*}{45}}\,\frac{T^2}{M_{\text{Pl}}},\qquad s\simeq\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_*T^3
    \]
    and $T=m_\chi/x$, we get:
    \begin{align*}
        RHS &= \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \frac{H}{s} \cdot x_{\mathrm{FO}} \\
        &= \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^3 g_*}{45}} \frac{T^2}{M_{\text{Pl}}}}{\frac{2\pi^2}{45} g_* T^3} \cdot x_{\mathrm{FO}} \\
        &= \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi g_*}} \cdot \frac{1}{ T M_{\text{Pl}} } \cdot x_{\mathrm{FO}} \\
        &= \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi g_*}} \cdot \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{ m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}} } \cdot x_{\mathrm{FO}} \\
        &= \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi g_*}} \cdot \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^2}{ m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}} }
    \end{align*}
    Now back to the LHS, we have
    \begin{align*}
        LHS &= Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}(x_{\mathrm{FO}}) \\
        &= \frac{45}{2\pi^2} \frac{g_\chi}{g_*} \left( \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{2\pi} \right)^{3/2} e^{-x_{\mathrm{FO}}}
    \end{align*}
    Equating the 2 sides and rearranging gives:
    \small
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{45}{2\pi^2} \frac{g_\chi}{g_*} \left( \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{2\pi} \right)^{3/2} e^{-x_{\mathrm{FO}}} &\simeq \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi g_*}} \cdot \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^2}{ m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}} } \\
        e^{-x_{\mathrm{FO}}} &\simeq \left[ \frac{1}{c(c+2) \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi g_*}} \cdot \frac{1}{m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}}} \right] \cdot \left[ \frac{2^{\frac{5}{2}}\pi^{\frac{7}{2}}}{45} \frac{g_*}{g_\chi} \right] \cdot \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^2}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^{3/2}} \\
        e^{x_{\mathrm{FO}}} &\simeq c(c+2) \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{8}} \frac{g_\chi}{2 \pi^3} \cdot \frac{m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}} \left( a + 6b / x_{\mathrm{FO}}  \right)}{\sqrt{g_*} \cdot \sqrt{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}}
    \end{align*}
    \normalsize
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The freeze-out temperature $T_{\mathrm{FO}}$ can be expressed as:
    \[
        x_{\mathrm{FO}} \equiv \frac{m_\chi}{T_{\mathrm{FO}}} \approx \ln \left[ c(c+2) \cdot \sqrt{\frac{45}{8}} \frac{g_\chi}{2 \pi^3} \cdot \frac{m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}} \left( a + 6b / x_{\mathrm{FO}}  \right)}{\sqrt{g_*} \cdot \sqrt{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}} \right]
    \]
    with $c \approx 0.5$ is a numerical determined quantity, $g_\chi$ is the number of internal degrees of freedom of the DM particle, $M_{\text{Pl}}$ is the Planck mass, $g_*$ is the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom at freeze-out, and $a$ and $b$ are the coefficients in the expansion of the thermally averaged annihilation cross section:
    \[
        \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle = a + b \langle v^2 \rangle + \mathcal{O}(\langle v^4 \rangle)
    \]
\end{theory}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{remark}
    For non-relativistic annihilation we expand
    \(\langle\sigma v\rangle=a+b\langle v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2\rangle+\cdots\).
    Equipartition for one particle gives $\langle v^2\rangle=3T/m_\chi=3/x$. For two equal-mass particles,
    $\langle v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2\rangle=\langle|\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2|^2\rangle=\langle v_1^2\rangle+\langle v_2^2\rangle=2\langle v^2\rangle=6/x$,
    hence $\langle\sigma v\rangle\simeq a+6b/x$.
\end{remark}


\bigskip \noindent
\begin{remark}
    \textbf{Typical value of $x_{\mathrm{FO}}\sim 20$--$30$.}\\
    The equation has the schematic form
    \[
        x_{\mathrm{FO}}\simeq \ln\!\left(\frac{A}{\sqrt{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}}\right),
        \qquad
        A\equiv c(c+2)\,\sqrt{\frac{45}{8}}\,\frac{g_\chi}{2\pi^3}\cdot \frac{m_\chi M_{\text{Pl}}\,\langle\sigma v\rangle}{\sqrt{g_*}}
    \]
    For WIMP-like parameters $g_\chi\sim\mathcal{O}(1)$, $g_*\sim 10^2$, $m_\chi\sim 10^2$--$10^3\,\mathrm{GeV}$, and a weak-scale annihilation rate
    $\langle\sigma v\rangle\sim 3\times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}\sim 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}$,
    the dimensionless combination $A$ is typically enormous ($A\sim 10^9$--$10^{13}$), so $\ln A\sim 20$--$30$.
    The extra $-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln x_{\mathrm{FO}}$ term only gives a mild (logarithmic) correction, so iterating once already lands in the same range.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relic Abundance}
%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}

    Now we derive an analytic estimate for the late-time comoving abundance $Y_\chi(\infty)$ and convert it into $\Omega_\chi h^2$. \\

    \textbf{1. Post freeze-out approximation.}\\
    Once $x\gtrsim x_{\mathrm{FO}}$, the equilibrium abundance becomes exponentially small, so $Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}\ll Y_\chi$. The Boltzmann equation
    \[
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} = -\frac{\lambda}{x^2}\left(Y_\chi^2-Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2\right)
    \]
    simplifies to
    \[
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx}\simeq -\frac{\lambda(x)}{x^2}Y_\chi^2
    \]
    Rewrite it as an equation for $1/Y_\chi$ by $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{Y_\chi}\right) = -\frac{1}{Y_\chi^2}\frac{dY_\chi}{dx}$, we get:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dY_\chi}{dx} &= -\frac{\lambda(x)}{x^2}Y_\chi^2 \\
        \Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{Y_\chi}\right) &= \frac{\lambda(x)}{x^2}
    \end{align*}
    Integrating from $x_{\mathrm{FO}}$ to $\infty$ gives
    \[
        \frac{1}{Y_\chi(\infty)} = \frac{1}{Y_\chi(x_{\mathrm{FO}})} + \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda(x')}{x'^2}\,dx'
    \]
    Note that present day abundance is much smaller than the abundance at freeze-out, so $Y_\chi(\infty)\ll Y_\chi(x_{\mathrm{FO}})$, which means $\frac{1}{Y_\chi(\infty)}\gg \frac{1}{Y_\chi(x_{\mathrm{FO}})}$, thus we can neglect the first term on the RHS and get:
    \[
        \frac{1}{Y_\chi(\infty)}\simeq \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda(x')}{x'^2}\,dx'
    \]

    \textbf{2. Include the $s$-wave and $p$-wave contributions.}\\
    For non-relativistic annihilation we expand
    \[
        \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle = a + b\,\langle v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2\rangle + \cdots
    \]
    Equipartition for one particle gives
    \[
        \frac{1}{2}m_\chi\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{3}{2}T
        \quad\Rightarrow\quad
        \langle v^2\rangle = \frac{3T}{m_\chi} = \frac{3}{x}
    \]
    For two equal-mass particles, $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathbf{v}_1-\mathbf{v}_2$ and $\langle \mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2\rangle=0$, hence
    \[
        \langle v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2\rangle = \langle v_1^2\rangle + \langle v_2^2\rangle = 2\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{6}{x}
    \]
    Therefore
    \[
        \langle\sigma_{\chi\bar\chi}v\rangle(x)\simeq a + \frac{6b}{x}
    \]

    \textbf{3. Perform the freeze-out integral explicitly.}\\
    Using $\lambda(x)=\sqrt{\pi/45}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi\,\langle\sigma v\rangle(x)$,
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{1}{Y_\chi(\infty)}
        &\simeq \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi
        \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle(x')}{x'^2}\,dx' \\
        &\simeq \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi
        \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{a}{x'^2}+\frac{6b}{x'^3}\right)dx'
    \end{align*}
    Evaluate each integral:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{cases}
            \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\frac{a}{x'^2}dx' &= \left[-\frac{a}{x'}\right]_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}=\frac{a}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}, \\
            \int_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}\frac{6b}{x'^3}dx' &= 6b\left[-\frac{1}{2x'^2}\right]_{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}^{\infty}=\frac{3b}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^2}
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    Thus
    \[
        \frac{1}{Y_\chi(\infty)}\simeq \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi\left(\frac{a}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}+\frac{3b}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}^2}\right)
        = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi\,\frac{a+3b/x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}
    \]
    Inverting,
    \[
        Y_\chi(\infty)\simeq \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{\sqrt{\pi/45}\,\sqrt{g_*}\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,m_\chi\left(a+3b/x_{\mathrm{FO}}\right)}
    \]

    \textbf{4. Convert $Y_\chi(\infty)$ to $\Omega_\chi h^2$.}\\
    Since $Y\equiv n/s$, the present number density is $n_{\chi,0}=s_0 Y_\chi(\infty)$ and
    \[
        \rho_{\chi,0}=m_\chi n_{\chi,0}=m_\chi s_0 Y_\chi(\infty)
    \]
    With $\Omega_\chi\equiv \rho_{\chi,0}/\rho_{c,0}$ and $\rho_{c,0}=3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$, we have
    \[
        \Omega_\chi h^2 = \frac{\rho_{\chi,0}}{\rho_{c,0}/h^2} = \frac{m_\chi s_0 Y_\chi(\infty)}{\rho_{c,0}/h^2}
    \]
    Using the standard present-day values
    \[
        s_0\simeq 2.9\times10^3\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}},\qquad \frac{\rho_{c,0}}{h^2}\simeq 1.05\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{GeV\,cm^{-3}}
    \]
    gives
    \[
        \Omega_\chi h^2\simeq 2.8\times10^8\left(\frac{m_\chi}{\mathrm{GeV}}\right)Y_\chi(\infty)
    \]
    Substituting $Y_\chi(\infty)$ shows the dependence on the annihilation cross section (the factor $m_\chi$ cancels):
    \[
        \Omega_\chi h^2 \propto \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{\sqrt{g_*}\,\left(a+3b/x_{\mathrm{FO}}\right)}
    \]
    After inserting numerical constants and expressing $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ in $\mathrm{cm^3/s}$, we obtain the practical estimate:
    \[
        \Omega_\chi h^2 \approx 0.1 \left( \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{20} \right) \left( \frac{g_*}{80} \right)^{-1/2} \left( \frac{a + 3b/x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{3 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}} \right)^{-1}
    \]
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The relic abundance of DM particles can be expressed as:
    \[
        \Omega_\chi h^2 \approx 0.1 \left( \frac{x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{20} \right) \left( \frac{g_*}{80} \right)^{-1/2} \left( \frac{a + 3b/x_{\mathrm{FO}}}{3 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}} \right)^{-1}
    \]
\end{theory}

The solid line in the figure represents the \textbf{Equilibrium Number Density} $Y_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$, while the dashed line represents the \textbf{Actual Number Density} $Y_\chi$. \\

    In the early universe, when $x$ is small, the dashed line closely follows the solid line, indicating that the DM particles are in thermal equilibrium with the thermal bath. As the universe expands and cools down, $T \ll m$ and $x$ increases, and the solid drops exponentially and tends to zero, when new particles cannot be created but remain particles can still annihilate. \\

    However, the dashed line does not drop to zero, and tends to a constant value(i.e. the relic density), the 2 lines separate at the freeze-out point ($m_chi / T \approx 20$), as the free-out stops the annihilation process continues, and the number density of DM particles is frozen at the value at freeze-out, which is much larger than the equilibrium number density at that time. \\

On the other hand, if $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle$ is large, the particles interact strongly, and they will continue to annihilate until a later time, which means the dashed line will separate from the solid line at a larger value of $x$, and the relic abundance will be smaller.
\end{extra}

\subsection{WIMP Miracle}
\begin{intuition}
    To get the relic abundance that we observed today, we need $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} v \rangle \approx 3 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}$. Interestingly, if we assume the particle is around the weak scale (i.e. $m_\chi \approx 100$~GeV, like W, Z bosons), and it interacts with the weak-force strength (i.e. $\alpha^2 / ( 100 \, \mathrm{GeV} )^2 \approx 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}$). This coincidence of alignment between the cosmologic observations and the particle physics scale is known as the WIMP miracle, which makes Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) a very attractive candidate for dark matter. And if the WIMP miracle is true, we should be able to detect WIMPs through their interactions with ordinary matter, which motivates the experimental searches for WIMPs in non-gravitational ways, such as direct detection, indirect detection, and collider detection.
\end{intuition}

\section{Non-gravitational Detections of Dark Matter}
The "WIMP miracle" shows the DM particles interact with regular matter with a strength comparable to the weak interaction, which means that we can search for DM particles through their non-gravitational interactions with ordinary matter. There are three main methods for non-gravitational detection of dark matter: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct Detection}: measuring the interactions between baryonic matter with DM particles.
    \item \textbf{Indirect Detection}: looking for the products of DM annihilation or decay (e.g. gamma rays, neutrinos, cosmic rays) in astrophysical observations.
    \item \textbf{Collider Detection}: searching for the production of DM particles in high-energy particle collisions (e.g. at the Large Hadron Collider), where DM particles would manifest as missing energy and momentum in the detector.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Direct Detection}
\begin{intuition}
    The basic idea of direct detection by E. Witten is to look for the elastic scattering between WIMP and nucleus in a detector. When a WIMP particle passes through the detector, it can interact with the nucleus of an atom in the detector material, causing the nucleus to recoil. By measuring the energy and direction of the recoiling nucleus, we can infer the presence of WIMPs and their properties (e.g. mass and cross section).
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}
    Start from the general formula for reaction rate:
    \[
        R_\text{total} = \text{(\# of target nuclei)} \times \underbrace{\text{(flux of incoming particles)} \times \text{(interaction prob)} }_{R_\text{per target}}
    \]
    We only consider the last 2 terms, start from the flux definition of a specific velocity $v$:
    \begin{align*}
        \text{flux} &= \text{(number density of incoming particles)} \times \text{(relative velocity)} \\
        &= \frac{\rho_\chi}{m_\chi} \cdot v f(\vec{v}) dv
    \end{align*}
    Then considering the interaction probability, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \text{interaction probability} &= \text{(differential cross section)} \\
        &= \frac{d\sigma}{d |\vec{q}|} d|\vec{q}|
    \end{align*}
    where $\vec{q}$ is the momentum transfer in the scattering process, and it shows the probability of a collision that transfers a specific amount of momentum $|\vec{q}|$ to the nucleus. The total interaction probability is obtained by integrating over all possible momentum transfers, which corresponds to integrating over the recoil energy of the nucleus. Thus we have:
    \begin{align*}
        R_\text{per target} &\approx \int_{E_{\mathrm{min}}}^{E_{\mathrm{max}}} \int_{v_{\mathrm{min}}}^{v_{\mathrm{max}}} \frac{\rho_\chi}{m_\chi} v f(\vec{v}) \frac{d\sigma}{d |\vec{q}|} dv d|\vec{q}| \\
        &\approx \int_{E_{\mathrm{min}}}^{E_{\mathrm{max}}} \int_{v_{\mathrm{min}}}^{v_{\mathrm{max}}} \frac{2 \rho_\chi}{m_\chi} \frac{d\sigma}{d |\vec{q}|} v f(\vec{v}) dv dE_R
    \end{align*}
    where factor of 2 term come from the Jacobian of the transformation from $|\vec{q}|$ to $E_R$ (i.e. $d|\vec{q}| = \sqrt{2 m_N E_R} dE_R$). \\

    To estimate the maximum recoil energy $E_{\mathrm{max}}$, we can start from the fact that it happens when the WIMP bounce back with the maximum momentum transfer $|\vec{q}|_{\mathrm{max}} = 2 \mu v$, where $\mu$ is the reduced mass of the WIMP-nucleus system, and $v$ is the relative velocity between the WIMP and the nucleus. Thus we have:
    \begin{align*}
        E_R^\mathrm{max} &= \frac{|\vec{q}|_{\mathrm{max}}^2}{2 m_N} \\
        &= \frac{(2 \mu v)^2}{2 m_N} \\
        &= \frac{2 \mu^2 v^2}{m_N} \\          
    \end{align*}
    Actually for a general recoil energy $E_R$, we have:
    \begin{align*}
        E_R &= \frac{|\vec{q}|^2}{2 m_N} \\
        &= \frac{(2 \mu^2 v^2 (1 - \cos\theta))}{2 m_N} \\
        &= \frac{m_\chi^2 m_N v^2 (1 - \cos\theta)}{(m_\chi + m_N)^2}
    \end{align*}
    where $\theta$ is the scattering angle in the center-of-mass frame, and the maximum recoil energy happens when $\theta = \pi$ (i.e. the WIMP bounce back). \\

    Now we can use the maximum recoil energy as upper limit of energy and hence get the minimum velocity $v_{\mathrm{min}}$ that can cause a recoil energy above the threshold of the detector:
    \begin{align*}
        E_R &\leq E_R^\mathrm{max} = \frac{2 \mu^2 v^2}{m_N} \\
        \frac{E_R m_N}{2 \mu^2} &\leq v^2 \\
        v &\geq \sqrt{\frac{E_R m_N}{2 \mu^2}} \equiv v_{\mathrm{min}}
    \end{align*}
    note it also depends on the recoil energy $E_R$, which means the higher the energy threshold of the detector, the higher the minimum velocity of WIMPs that can be detected.
\end{foundation}

%\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The rate of the WIMP-nucleus scattering events in a direct detection experiment can be expressed as:
    \[
        R_\text{per target} \approx \int_{E_{\mathrm{min}}}^{E_{\mathrm{max}}} \int_{v_{\mathrm{min}}}^{v_{\mathrm{max}}} \frac{2 \rho_\chi}{m_\chi} \frac{d\sigma}{d |\vec{q}|} v f(\vec{v}) dv dE_R
    \]
    Where $\frac{2 \rho_\chi}{m_\chi}$ is the local number density of WIMPs, $\frac{d\sigma}{d |\vec{q}|}$ is the differential cross section for WIMP-nucleus scattering, $v$ is the relative velocity between the WIMP and the nucleus, $f(\vec{v})$ is the velocity distribution of WIMPs in the galactic halo, and $E_R$ is the recoil energy of the nucleus. The limits of integration $E_{\mathrm{min}}$, $E_{\mathrm{max}}$, $v_{\mathrm{min}}$, and $v_{\mathrm{max}}$ depend on the specific experimental setup and the properties of the WIMP and target nucleus. \\
    
    In actual case, $v_{\mathrm{max}}$ is typically taken as the escape velocity of the galaxy $\sim 650$ km/s, and $v_{\mathrm{min}}$ is determined by the kinematics of the scattering process and the energy threshold of the detector $\sim (E_R m_N / 2 \mu^2)^{1/2}$, and is around $1 \sim 100 \text{keV}$. \\

    And the recoil energy is given by:
    \[
        E_R = \frac{|\vec{q}|^2}{2 m_N} = \frac{2 \mu^2 v^2 (1 - \cos\theta)}{2 m_N} = \frac{m_\chi^2 m_N v^2 (1 - \cos\theta)}{(m_\chi + m_N)^2}
    \]
    where $\vec{q}$ is the momentum of WIMP, $v$ is velocity, $\mu$ is the reduced mass of the WIMP-nucleus system, and it is obvious if we want to maximize the recoil energy, we need $m_\chi \approx m_N$ (try let $m_N = k m_\chi$ and take the derivative!), which means the WIMP mass should be around the mass of the target nucleus to get the best sensitivity in direct detection experiments. \\
    
    However, as we dont know the actual mass of the WIMP, we need to use a variety of target materials to cover a wide range of possible WIMP masses (as in below figure).
\end{theory}

\begin{foundation}
    Now we derive the spin-independent elastic scattering cross section used in direct-detection experiments. The goal is to connect a microscopic WIMP--quark interaction to the effective WIMP--nucleus cross section measured by experiments. \\

    \textbf{1. Start from the physical picture of elastic scattering.}\\
    In direct detection, the incoming WIMP $\chi$ hits a nucleus $N_A$ in the detector,
    \[
        \chi + N_A \rightarrow \chi + N_A,
    \]
    where $N_A$ denotes the whole nucleus with atomic number $Z$ and mass number $A$. ``Elastic'' means the particles after collision are still the same particles as before collision: no new particles are created, and the nucleus is not broken apart. The only observable effect is that the nucleus recoils with a small recoil energy $E_R$. \\

    In the low-energy limit relevant for underground detectors, the de Broglie wavelength of the momentum transfer is usually larger than, or comparable to, the size of the nucleus. Therefore the WIMP cannot resolve the detailed positions of individual protons and neutrons. Instead, the scattering amplitudes from all nucleons add \textbf{coherently}. This is the origin of the important $A^2$ enhancement. \\

    \textbf{2. Add the amplitudes from protons and neutrons.}\\
    Let $f_p$ be the effective coupling between the WIMP and one proton, and $f_n$ be the effective coupling between the WIMP and one neutron. A nucleus contains
    \[
        Z \quad \text{protons}, \qquad A-Z \quad \text{neutrons}.
    \]
    Since spin-independent scattering does not care about the spin direction, and since the scattering is coherent, the total effective coupling to the whole nucleus is the sum of the proton and neutron contributions:
    \begin{align*}
        f_A &= \underbrace{f_p+f_p+\cdots+f_p}_{Z\ \text{times}}
        +\underbrace{f_n+f_n+\cdots+f_n}_{A-Z\ \text{times}} \\
        &= Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n .
    \end{align*}
    This is an \textbf{amplitude-level} sum, not a probability-level sum. Therefore, when we calculate the cross section, the square will appear:
    \[
        |f_A|^2 = \left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2 .
    \]

    \textbf{3. Include the two-body kinematics through the reduced mass.}\\
    For a two-body collision, the relevant inertia is not simply $m_\chi$ or $m_A$ alone, but the reduced mass
    \[
        \mu_A \equiv \frac{m_\chi m_A}{m_\chi+m_A},
    \]
    where $m_A$ is the mass of the whole target nucleus. This is the same reduced-mass idea as in the recoil-energy derivation above: if one particle is much heavier than the other, the lighter particle controls the motion; if the two masses are comparable, the momentum transfer is most efficient. \\

    In the standard direct-detection normalization for a short-range contact interaction, the total non-relativistic elastic cross section is
    \[
        \sigma^{\mathrm{SI}} = \frac{4\mu_A^2}{\pi}|f_A|^2 .
    \]
    The factor $\mu_A^2$ comes from two-body kinematics, while the factor $|f_A|^2$ comes from the usual rule that a probability is proportional to the square of the amplitude. Substituting the coherent nuclear coupling gives
    \begin{align*}
        \sigma^{\mathrm{SI}}
        &= \frac{4\mu_A^2}{\pi}\left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2 \\
        &= \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{m_\chi m_A}{m_\chi+m_A}\right)^2
        \left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2 .
    \end{align*}
    This is the elastic scattering cross section quoted below. Notice that the cross section grows strongly with the size of the nucleus because the amplitudes from many nucleons can add before squaring. \\

    \textbf{4. Derive the nucleon coupling $f_{p,n}$ from quark couplings.}\\
    The WIMP does not directly see a proton or neutron as an elementary object. A proton or neutron is made of quarks and gluons. At low energy, we describe the WIMP--quark scalar interaction by an effective coupling $a_q$ to each quark $q$:
    \[
        \chi \ \text{couples to} \ q \quad \text{with strength} \quad a_q .
    \]
    Since the nucleon is a bound state, we need the expectation value of the quark operator inside a proton or neutron. The useful definition is
    \[
        f_{T_q}^{(p,n)} \equiv \frac{\langle p,n|m_q \bar{q}q|p,n\rangle}{m_{p,n}} .
    \]
    In words, $f_{T_q}^{(p,n)}$ is the fraction of the proton or neutron mass that comes from the light quark $q$. Rearranging this definition gives
    \[
        \langle p,n|\bar{q}q|p,n\rangle
        = f_{T_q}^{(p,n)}\frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q} .
    \]
    Therefore, the contribution of the light quarks $u,d,s$ to the effective WIMP--nucleon coupling is
    \[
        f_{p,n}^{\mathrm{light}}
        = \sum_{q=u,d,s} a_q \langle p,n|\bar{q}q|p,n\rangle
        = \sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(p,n)} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q} .
    \]

    \textbf{5. Include the heavy-quark contribution through gluons.}\\
    Heavy quarks $c,b,t$ are not ordinary valence quarks inside the proton or neutron. However, they can still affect WIMP--nucleon scattering indirectly through loop diagrams that couple to gluons. The remaining fraction of the nucleon mass not carried by the light quarks is written as
    \[
        f_{TG}^{(p,n)} \equiv 1-\sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(p,n)} .
    \]
    Using the standard heavy-quark matching result from QCD, each heavy quark contributes
    \[
        f_{p,n}^{\mathrm{heavy}}
        = \frac{2}{27} f_{TG}^{(p,n)} \sum_{q=c,b,t} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q} .
    \]
    The numerical factor $2/27$ is a QCD result. For this course, the important point is the physical meaning: heavy quarks contribute mainly by modifying the gluon content of the nucleon, not by appearing as ordinary constituent quarks. \\

    \textbf{6. Combine light and heavy contributions.}\\
    Adding the light-quark part and the heavy-quark/gluon part gives
    \begin{align*}
        f_{p,n}
        &= f_{p,n}^{\mathrm{light}} + f_{p,n}^{\mathrm{heavy}} \\
        &= \sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(p,n)} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q}
        + \frac{2}{27} f_{TG}^{(p,n)} \sum_{q=c,b,t} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q} .
    \end{align*}
    Hence the final cross section depends on two levels of physics:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item particle physics: the WIMP--quark couplings $a_q$;
        \item nuclear physics: how quarks and gluons contribute to the nucleon mass, encoded in $f_{T_q}^{(p,n)}$ and $f_{TG}^{(p,n)}$.
    \end{itemize}



\end{foundation}

\begin{theory}
    The spin-independent WIMP-nucleon elastic scattering cross section is given by:
    \[
        \sigma \approx \frac{4 \mu^2}{\pi} \left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2 = \frac{4 m_\chi^2 m_N^2}{\pi (m_\chi + m_N)^2} \left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2
    \]
    where $Z$ and $A$ are the atomic number and atomic mass of nucleus respectively, $f_p$ and $f_n$ are the effective couplings of WIMP to proton and neutron respectively, which can be expressed as:
    \[
        f_{p,n} = \sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(p,n)} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q} + \frac{2}{27} f_{TG}^{(p,n)} \sum_{q=c,b,t} a_q \frac{m_{p,n}}{m_q}
    \]
    where $a_q$ is the effective coupling of WIMP to quark $q$, $f_{T_q}^{(p,n)}$ is the fraction of the nucleon mass that is contributed by the light quark $q$, and $f_{TG}^{(p,n)}$ is the fraction of the nucleon mass that is contributed by the gluons. The 1st term corresponds to interactions with the quarks in the target nuclei, while the 2nd term corresponds to interactions with the gluons in the target nuclei, which is induced by the heavy quarks (i.e. $c$, $b$, and $t$) through loop diagrams. \\
    
    By measurement in nuclear physic experiments, we have the below approximate values:
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{c|r}
            \hline
            $f_{T_u}^{(p)}$ & $0.020 \pm 0.004$ \\
            $f_{T_d}^{(p)}$ & $0.026 \pm 0.005$ \\
            $f_{T_s}^{(p)}$ & $0.118 \pm 0.062$ \\
            \hline
            $f_{T_u}^{(n)}$ & $0.014 \pm 0.003$ \\
            $f_{T_d}^{(n)}$ & $0.036 \pm 0.008$ \\
            $f_{T_s}^{(n)}$ & $0.118 \pm 0.062$ \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    And $f_{TG}^{(p)} = 1 - \sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(p)} \approx 0.84$, $f_{TG}^{(n)} = 1 - \sum_{q=u,d,s} f_{T_q}^{(n)} \approx 0.83$. \\

\end{theory}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    Further, if we use the isospin-symmetry (i.e. $f_p \approx f_n$), then we can express the WIMP-nucleon cross section as:
    \begin{align*}
        \sigma &\approx \frac{4 \mu^2}{\pi} \left[ Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n \right]^2 \\
        &= \frac{4 \mu^2}{\pi} A^2 f_n^2 \\
        &= \left( \frac{4}{\pi} m_N^2 f_n^2 \right) \frac{\mu^2}{m_N^2} A^2 \\
        &= \sigma_n \frac{\mu^2}{m_N^2} A^2 \\
        &= f(\sigma_n, m_\chi)
    \end{align*}
    The $\sigma_n$ is the cross section between WIMP and nucleon.
\end{theory}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{importantnote}
    Some may mix the cross section of WIMP-nucleon scattering $\sigma_n$ with the cross section between WIMP and single nucleon $\sigma$, but they are different quantities, and their relationship is given by above. \\

    More carefully, $\sigma$ is the cross section between a WIMP and the entire nucleus, while $\sigma_n$ is the reference cross section between a WIMP and one neutron (or one nucleon under the isospin-symmetric approximation $f_p\simeq f_n$). Direct-detection exclusion plots usually show this per-nucleon reference cross section so that experiments with different target nuclei can be compared on the same plot.
\end{importantnote}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{remark}
    To avoid the background noise from cosmic rays and other sources, direct detection experiments are typically conducted deep underground (e.g. in mines or underground laboratories) to shield them from cosmic rays and other sources of background radiation. The detectors are also designed to be highly sensitive and have low energy thresholds to detect the small recoil energies expected from WIMP interactions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Indirect Detection: Gamma Rays}
\begin{intuition}
    Another way to search for DM particles is to look for the products of WIMP annihilation or decay in astrophysical observations. If DM particles can annihilate or decay into Standard Model particles, they can produce high-energy gamma rays, neutrinos, or cosmic rays e.t.c that can be detected by telescopes and detectors on Earth or in space. \\

    One of the most common detection is the gamma rays from WIMP annihilation, it has 2 main advantages compared to other indirect detection methods:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gamma rays are not deflected by magnetic fields, so they can point back to their source, which allows us to identify potential DM-rich regions (e.g. the center of our galaxy, dwarf spheroidal galaxies, galaxy clusters) where the DM density is expected to be high, and look for excess gamma-ray emission from these regions that could be attributed to DM annihilation.
        \item The diffusion effect is small for gamma rays, which means the spectrum we observe is close to the original spectrum produced by DM annihilation, which can provide more information about the properties of DM particles (e.g. mass and annihilation channels).
    \end{itemize}
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}
    Note the number of Dm annihilation per unit time and unit volume:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{d N_\text{ann}}{dt dV} &= \frac{1}{2} \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle n_\chi^2
    \end{align*}
    where the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ is to avoid double counting, because each annihilation involves two DM particles. \\

    Then given that each annihilation produces a spectrum of gamma rays by $\frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma}$, the source term (photon production per unit energy, time, and volume) can be expressed as:
    \begin{align*}
        Q(E_\gamma, \vec{r}) &= \frac{1}{2} \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle n_\chi^2(\vec{r}) \cdot \frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma} \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle \frac{\rho^2(\vec{r}, \psi)}{m_\chi^2} \cdot \frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma}
    \end{align*}
    And hence the flux is:
    \begin{align*}
        \Phi_\gamma(E_\gamma, \psi) &= \frac{1}{4 \pi} \int_{\text{l.o.s}} Q(E_\gamma, \vec{r}) dl(\psi) \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle \cdot \frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma} \frac{1}{4\pi m_\chi^2} \int_{\text{l.o.s}} \rho^2(r, \psi) dl(\psi)
    \end{align*}
    The first part outside the integral is called the "particle physics factor", which depends on the properties of DM particles (e.g. mass, annihilation cross section, and annihilation channels), and the second part inside the integral is called the "astrophysical factor" or "J-factor", which depends on the distribution of DM in the target region observed.
\end{foundation} 

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The gamma-ray spectrum from WIMP annihilation can be expressed as:
    \[
        \Phi_\gamma(E_\gamma, \psi) = \frac{1}{2} \langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle \cdot \frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma} \frac{1}{4\pi m_\chi^2} \int_{\text{l.o.s}} \rho^2(r, \psi) dl(\psi)
    \]
    where $\Phi_\gamma(E_\gamma, \psi)$ is the gamma-ray flux at energy $E_\gamma$ and direction $\psi$, $\langle \sigma_{\chi \bar{\chi}} |v| \rangle$ is the thermally averaged annihilation cross section times velocity, $\frac{dN_\gamma}{dE_\gamma}$ is the gamma-ray spectrum per annihilation, $m_\chi$ is the mass of the DM particle, $\rho(r, \psi)$ is the DM density distribution as a function of distance $r$ and direction $\psi$, and the integral is taken along the line of sight (l.o.s) in the direction $\psi$.
\end{theory}

    For each graph, we can see there is different colored lines, they represent different theoretical spectrum of gamma rays produced by WIMP annihilation through different channels (i.e. different annihilation products). It can be divided into 3 categories: \\

    The left side is the "soft" spectrum (\textbf{Hadronic channels}), including the solid cyan line ($b \bar{b}$ channel), the magenta dashed line ($Z Z$ channel), and the blue dashed line ($W^+ W^-$ channel). When WIMPs annihilate into these particles, they are unstable and will quickly decay into jets of hadrons (e.g. pions $pi$), and then the neutral pions ($\pi^0$) will decay almost instantly into 2 gamma rays $\pi^0 \to \gamma \gamma$. In above hadronization process, original energy shared among many particles in jet, resulting photons have wide range of energies, which produce a "soft" spectrum that peaks at lower energies. However, they tend to be on lower side of mass(left side of graph), because the energy is shared among many particles, so the photons have less energy than the original WIMP mass. \\

    The right side is the "hard" spectrum (\textbf{Leptonic channels}), including the red dashed line ($\tau^+ \tau^-$ channel), and the green dashed line ($\mu^+ \mu^-$ channel). When WIMPs annihilate into leptons (e.g. $\tau$ and $\mu$), they can produce gamma rays through final state radiation (FSR) or internal bremsstrahlung, instead of undergoing hadronization. Since the leptons are not hadrons, they do not produce jets of particles, and the gamma rays produced from leptonic channels tend to have energies closer to the original WIMP mass, resulting in a "hard" spectrum that peaks at higher energies. Furthermore, the FSR and bremsstrahlung processes is much less rare than the hadronization process, which means the leptonic channels produce fewer gamma rays compared to hadronic channels, resulting in a lower overall intensity of the gamma-ray spectrum. Besides, due to conservation of energy, the gamma rays produced from leptonic channels cannot have energies greater than the WIMP mass, which is why the spectrum cuts off at the WIMP mass in the right side of the graph. \\

    The solid back line between the "soft" and "hard" spectrum is the $\tau^+ \tau^-$ channel, which is a special case because the $\tau$ lepton can decay into hadrons about 65\% of the time, it resulting a mixture of both hadronic and leptonic channels, which produces a spectrum that has characteristics of both "soft" and "hard" spectra, with a peak that is intermediate between the two. \\

    Comparing the left and right graph, we can see that the spectrum for 500 GeV WIMP mass is similar to the spectrum for 100 GeV WIMP mass, but it is shifted to higher energies, which is expected because the energy of the gamma rays produced from WIMP annihilation is related to the mass of the WIMP, so heavier WIMPs will produce higher energy gamma rays. Meanwhile, the shape of the spectrum remains similar, and is determined by the properties of the annihilation channels, rather than the WIMP mass itself.
\end{extra}



\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    Apart from the continuos spectrum shown in above figure, there is also a possibility of producing a "line" spectrum, which is a sharp peak at a specific energy corresponding to the WIMP mass ("smoking gun"). This can occur if WIMPs annihilate directly produce gamma rays directly. \\

    In such case, WIMPs produce final states like $\gamma \gamma$ or $\gamma Z$ or $\gamma h$, through \textbf{loop-level processes}, and the final states radiation has energy of $E_\gamma = m_\chi$ for $\gamma \gamma$ channel, and $E_\gamma = m_\chi (1 - m_Z^2 / 4 m_\chi^2)$ for $\gamma Z$ channel, and $E_\gamma = m_\chi (1 - m_h^2 / 4 m_\chi^2)$ for $\gamma h$ channel respectively. \\

    Overall, the galactic center is the most promising target for gamma-ray searches for DM annihilation, because it is expected to have the highest DM density, and hence the highest annihilation rate. 
\end{intuition}


\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}
    We derive the monochromatic photon energy from the 2-body annihilation kinematics of non-relativistic WIMPs $\chi\chi \to \gamma X$, where $X = \gamma, Z, h$. \\

    Recall energy-momentum conservation in special relativity:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{cases}
            E_\chi + E_\chi &= E_\gamma + E_X \\
            \vec{p}_\chi + \vec{p}_\chi &= \vec{p}_\gamma + \vec{p}_X
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    For non-relativistic WIMPs, we can approximate $E_\chi \approx m_\chi$ and $\vec{p}_\chi \approx 0$, so the equations simplify to:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{cases}
            2 m_\chi &= E_\gamma + E_X \\
            \vec{p}_\gamma &= - \vec{p}_X
        \end{cases}
    \end{align*}
    Combine the two equations, and substitute $E_\gamma = |\vec{p}_\gamma|$ and $E_X = \sqrt{|\vec{p}_X|^2 + m_X^2}$, we get:
    \begin{align*}
        2 m_\chi &= |\vec{p}_\gamma| + \sqrt{|\vec{p}_\gamma|^2 + m_X^2} \\
        4 m_\chi^2 - 4 m_\chi |\vec{p}_\gamma| + |\vec{p}_\gamma|^2 &= m_X^2 + |\vec{p}_\gamma|^2 \\
        4 m_\chi^2 - 4 m_\chi |\vec{p}_\gamma| &= m_X^2 \\
        |\vec{p}_\gamma| &= m_\chi \left(1 - \frac{m_X^2}{4 m_\chi^2}\right)
    \end{align*}
    Note in above equation, if $X = \gamma$, then $m_X = m_\gamma = 0$. \\

    The 3 possible loop-level annihilation channels and their corresponding photon energies are:
    \begin{align*}
        \chi\chi \to \gamma\gamma &: \quad E_\gamma = m_\chi \\
        \chi\chi \to \gamma Z &: \quad E_\gamma = m_\chi \left(1 - \frac{m_Z^2}{4m_\chi^2}\right) \\
        \chi\chi \to \gamma h &: \quad E_\gamma = m_\chi \left(1 - \frac{m_h^2}{4m_\chi^2}\right)
    \end{align*}
    For WIMP masses $m_\chi \gtrsim 100\,\mathrm{GeV}$, the $\gamma Z$ and $\gamma h$ lines are shifted slightly below $m_\chi$ due to the finite masses of the $Z$ boson ($m_Z \approx 91.2\,\mathrm{GeV}$) and the Higgs boson ($m_h \approx 125\,\mathrm{GeV}$). These monochromatic lines serve as a ``smoking gun'' signature for DM annihilation because no known astrophysical background can produce a sharp, line-like feature at such energies.
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{example}{Loop-level Processes v.s. Tree-level Processes}{}
    Why in line spectrum case, the WIMPs produce gamma rays through loop-level processes, instead of tree-level processes? \\

    \bigskip\textbf{Solution.}\quad \\
    The neutral WIMP, being electrically neutral, cannot couple directly to photons at tree level due to electromagnetic gauge invariance; thus $\chi\chi\to\gamma\gamma$ or $\gamma Z$ must proceed via loops of virtual charged states.  \\

    \textbf{WIMPs vs. EWIMPs:} Generic WIMPs may have heavy or decoupled charged mediators, yielding highly suppressed loop amplitudes. In contrast, \textbf{EWIMPs} (e.g. pure wino/higgsino) belong to electroweak multiplets with \textit{near-degenerate charged partners}; these light charged states run in the loop, enhancing the line cross-section relative to generic WIMPs—though still loop-suppressed compared to tree-level continuum channels. The monochromatic signal persists in both cases because the final-state photon energy is fixed by kinematics, unlike the broad spectrum from tree-level hadronic cascades.
\end{example}

\subsection{Indirect Detection: Charged Cosmic Rays}
\begin{intuition}
    In addition to gamma rays, WIMP annihilation can also produce charged cosmic rays (e.g. positrons, antiprotons, electrons, and protons) that can be detected by cosmic ray detectors (e.g. AMS-02 on the International Space Station). However, the detection of charged cosmic rays from WIMP annihilation is more challenging than gamma rays due to the following reasons:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Charged cosmic rays are deflected by magnetic fields in the galaxy, which diffuse and losing energy, resulting a diffuse spectrum observed at Earth. Hence, we identify the signatures of DM annihilation by study wether the spectrum is distorted and loss angular information, instead of the spectrum amplitude like gamma rays. \\
    \end{itemize}
    
    Compared to antiprotons and antideuterons, positrons are more attractive for DM detection, as it lost most of their energy over a short distance (few kpc or less), the positron spectrum observed at Earth is sampled by local DM distribution and hence less affected by the uncertainties in the DM distribution in the galaxy. They propagate under the influence of the galactic magnetic field, losing energy through processes such as synchrotron emission and inverse Compton scattering, and finally reach the Earth with a spectrum.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{foundation}

    The diffusion-loss equation is essentially a \textbf{continuity equation} (number conservation) but written for the \textbf{number density per unit energy}. Below are the steps to derive the diffusion-loss equation: \\

    \textbf{Step 1: Define the quantity we evolve.} \\
    Let
    \[
        \psi(E_e, \vec{x}, t) \equiv \frac{d n_e}{dE_e}(E_e, \vec{x}, t)
    \]
    be the positron number density per unit energy. Its unit is $[\psi] = \#\,\mathrm{(energy)}^{-1}\,\mathrm{(volume)}^{-1}$. \\

    Consider an arbitrary fixed spatial volume $V$ bounded by surface $S$, and an arbitrary energy interval $[E_1,E_2]$. The total number of positrons inside $V$ whose energies lie in $[E_1,E_2]$ is
    \[
        N_{[E_1,E_2]}(t) = \int_V d^3x \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \psi(E_e, \vec{x}, t)
    \]
    Taking a time derivative (the integration limits are fixed) gives
    \[
        \frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt} = \int_V d^3x \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}(E_e, \vec{x}, t)
    \]

    \textbf{Step 2: Account for transport in real space.} \\
    Let $\vec{J}(E_e, \vec{x}, t)$ be the spatial number \textbf{current density per unit energy} (units: $\#\,\mathrm{(energy)}^{-1}\,\mathrm{(area)}^{-1}\,\mathrm{time}^{-1}$). The net loss of particles from the volume due to spatial transport is the outward flux through the boundary:
    \[
        \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm space}
        = - \oint_S d\vec{S}\cdot \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \vec{J}(E_e, \vec{x}, t)
    \]
    Apply the divergence theorem (and swap the order of integrals):
    \begin{align*}
        - \oint_S d\vec{S}\cdot \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \vec{J}
        &= - \int_V d^3x\, \vec{\nabla}\cdot \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \vec{J} \\
        &= - \int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \vec{\nabla}\cdot \vec{J}(E_e, \vec{x}, t)
    \end{align*}

    \textbf{Step 3: Account for energy loss as a flux in energy space.} \\
    Positrons lose energy continuously (synchrotron, inverse Compton, etc.). Write the energy change rate as
    \[
        \dot{E}_e \equiv \frac{dE_e}{dt}
    \]
    If there is no spatial motion and no sources, particles simply \emph{flow} along the energy axis: they cross the upper boundary $E_2$ into lower energies, and they also cross the lower boundary $E_1$ and leave the interval.

    In a short time $dt$, the number of particles crossing an energy boundary $E$ per unit volume is
    \[
        \left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)\,dt
    \]
    (this is the "flux" in energy space; it is exactly analogous to a spatial flux $\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}$). Therefore, the net change of particle number inside the energy interval due to energy drift is
    \begin{align*}
        \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm loss}
        &= -\int_V d^3x\, \Big[\dot{E}_e\,\psi(E_e,\vec{x},t)\Big]_{E_1}^{E_2} \\
        &= -\int_V d^3x\, \left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\big|_{E_2} - \dot{E}_e\,\psi\big|_{E_1}\right)
    \end{align*}
    Use the fundamental theorem of calculus, $f(E_2)-f(E_1)=\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial E}$, with $f(E_e)=\dot{E}_e\,\psi$:
    \begin{align*}
        -\Big[\dot{E}_e\,\psi\Big]_{E_1}^{E_2}
        &= -\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)
    \end{align*}
    Hence
    \[
        \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm loss}
        = -\int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)
    \]

    \textbf{Step 4: Add a source term.} \\
    Let $Q(E_e,\vec{x})$ be the injection rate (number produced per unit energy, volume, and time). Then
    \[
        \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm source} = \int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, Q(E_e, \vec{x})
    \]

    \textbf{Step 5: Combine all contributions and localize the equation.} \\
    Putting Step 2--4 together,
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}
        &= \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm space}
        + \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm loss}
        + \left.\frac{dN_{[E_1,E_2]}}{dt}\right|_{\rm source} \\
        \int_V d^3x \int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
        &= -\int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \vec{\nabla}\cdot \vec{J}
        -\int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right) \\
        & +\int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, Q
    \end{align*}
    Move everything to one side and factor out the integrals:
    \[
        \int_V d^3x\int_{E_1}^{E_2} dE_e\, \left[
            \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
            + \vec{\nabla}\cdot \vec{J}
            + \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)
            - Q
        \right] = 0
    \]
    Since $V$ and $[E_1,E_2]$ are arbitrary, the integrand must vanish pointwise:
    \[
        \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
        = -\vec{\nabla}\cdot \vec{J}
        - \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)
        + Q
    \]

    \textbf{Step 6: Diffusion approximation and the sign convention for energy loss.} \\
    In a turbulent magnetic field, charged particles undergo a random walk, so the spatial transport is modeled as diffusion (Fick's law):
    \[
        \vec{J}(E_e,\vec{x},t) = -K(E_e,\vec{x})\, \vec{\nabla}\psi(E_e,\vec{x},t)
    \]
    where $K$ is the diffusion coefficient (units: $[K]=\mathrm{length}^2/\mathrm{time}$). Substituting gives
    \[
        -\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J} = \vec{\nabla}\cdot\left[K(E_e,\vec{x})\,\vec{\nabla}\psi\right]
    \]
    For energy losses, it is conventional to define a \textbf{positive} loss function
    \[
        b(E_e,\vec{x}) \equiv -\dot{E}_e = -\frac{dE_e}{dt} \quad (>0\ \text{for energy loss})
    \]
    so that
    \[
        -\frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(\dot{E}_e\,\psi\right)
        = -\frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(-b\,\psi\right)
        = \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e}\left(b\,\psi\right)
    \]
    Putting these together and recalling $\psi=\frac{dn_e}{dE_e}$ yields exactly the diffusion-loss equation used below:
    \[
        \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \frac{d n_e}{dE_e}
        = \vec{\nabla} \cdot \left[ K(E_e, \vec{x}) \vec{\nabla} \frac{d n_e}{dE_e} \right]
        + \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e} \left[ b(E_e, \vec{x}) \frac{d n_e}{dE_e} \right]
        + Q(E_e, \vec{x})
    \]
\end{foundation}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{theory}
    The resulting spectrum at Earth can be calculated by solving the diffusion-loss equation:
    \[
        \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \frac{d n_e}{dE_e} = \vec{\nabla} \cdot \left[ K(E_e, \vec{x}) \vec{\nabla} \frac{d n_e}{dE_e} \right] + \frac{\partial}{\partial E_e} \left[ b(E_e, \vec{x}) \frac{d n_e}{dE_e} \right] + Q(E_e, \vec{x})
    \]
    where $\frac{d n_e}{dE_e}$ is the number density of positrons per unit energy, $K(E_e, \vec{x})$ is the diffusion coefficient, $b(E_e, \vec{x})$ is the energy loss rate, and $Q(E_e, \vec{x})$ is the source term, which includes the information regarding the DM annihilation modes, cross section, and DM distribution in the galaxy. By solving this equation with appropriate boundary conditions, we can predict the positron spectrum at Earth resulting from DM annihilation, and compare it with the observed spectrum to look for potential signatures of DM.
\end{theory}

\subsection{Collider Detection}
\begin{intuition}
    Further, we can using a particle accelerator (e.g. LHC in CERN) to create WIMP particle by colliding high-energy particles together. Since WIMPs are weakly interacting and electrically neutral, they would not leave a direct signal in the detector, but we can infer their existence by looking for missing energy and momentum in the collision events. Given that the initial momentum conserved in the \textbf{transverse plane} (perpendicular to the beam axis) is zero:
    \[
        \sum_{\text{vis}} p_{\perp} + \sum_{\text{invis}} p_{\perp} = 0
    \]
    Then, if after collision:
    \[
        \sum_{\text{vis}} p_{\perp} \neq 0
    \]
    i.e. the summation of the transverse momentum of all visible particles is not zero, it indicates that there are invisible particles (e.g. WIMPs) carrying away the missing transverse momentum, which can be a signal for the production of dark matter particles in the collider:
    \[
        \text{Missing Transverse Momentum} = - \sum_{\text{vis}} p_{\perp} = \sum_{\text{invis}} p_{\perp}
    \]
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{intuition}
    Some may ask how about using the momentum along the beam axis (i.e. \textbf{longitudinal momentum})? Actually conservation of longitudinal momentum is not useful for detecting WIMPs in collider experiments. \\
    
    In the accelerator, the colliding particles (e.g. protons) are composite particles made up of quarks and gluons, and each of these constituent partons can carry a different fraction of the proton's total momentum. Further, the actual hard collision happens between partons(e.g. gluons and quarks) inside the protons, not the protons themselves \cite{PhysicsGurus_Energies_Accelerator_Collision}, hence:
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{\text{vis}} p_{\parallel} &\neq 0 \\
        | \sum_{\text{vis}} p_{\parallel} + \sum_{\text{invis}} p_{\parallel} | & \gg 0
    \end{align*}
    As a result, Even we can assume the initial longitudinal momentum of the colliding partons is zero, but we cannot precisely determine the initial longitudinal momentum of the system, and we cant tell the non-zero longitudinal momentum of the visible particles is due to the production of WIMPs or just due to the initial momentum of the partons. Therefore, we focus on the missing transverse momentum, which is well-defined and can be used to infer the presence of invisible particles like WIMPs.
\end{intuition}

\bigskip \noindent
\begin{remark}
    You may ask why the \textbf{transverse momentum} doesn't have the same problem as the \textbf{longitudinal momentum}? Actually, the initial transverse momentum of the colliding partons is effectively zero because the protons are accelerated along the beam axis, and any transverse momentum they have(and their constituent partons have) is negligible compared to the longitudinal momentum. Therefore, any significant missing transverse momentum observed in the detector can be attributed to the production of invisible particles like WIMPs, rather than uncertainties in the initial state of the collision.
\end{remark}

\end{importantnote}

The current search often need combine the results from multiple detection methods (e.g. direct, indirect, and collider) to constrain the properties of dark matter particles and to rule out certain models.
\end{extra}

\subsection{Theoretical Candidates}
%\bigskip \noindent

\begin{extra}
    The above figure show some of the hypothetical candidates for dark matter, which x-axis is the mass of the candidate in different energy scale, and y-axis is the interaction strength of the candidate with ordinary matter. The most bottom of y-axis is $G_N$, which means the candidate only interact with ordinary matter through gravity, and when we go up, the particles interact stronger (e.g. via weak nuclear force, or "new dark force"). \\

    We can see that the graph divided into 3 regions, lets start with the \textbf{left region} first, which is the region of Wave-like DM candidates, which are very light particles with very weak interactions. The Paulin exclusion principle prevents these particles from being packed too closely together, so they must be the bosons, and they act like fluid or field and have wave-like properties, they also have very large de Broglie wavelength, which can be comparable to the size of galaxies. Some key candidates in this region include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{QCD Axions}: These are hypothetical particles that arise from the Peccei-Quinn solution to the strong CP problem in quantum chromodynamics (QCD). It locate the transition between the wave-like DM and particle-like DM.
        \item \textbf{Fuzzy DM}: These are ultra-light bosonic particles that behave like oscillating field.
    \end{itemize}
    \\

    The \textbf{right region} is the region of Particle-like DM candidates, which are heavier particles with shorter de Broglie wavelength, and they behave more like distinct particles rather than a fluid. Some key candidates in this region include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{WIMPs}: The classic "Holy Grail" of dark matter candidates, sit in the $GeV$ to $TeV$ mass range, and interact with weak-force strength.
        \item \textbf{Sterile Neutrinos ($\nu_s$ DM )}: A heavier cousin of the known neutrinos, these particles are "warm" dark matter candidates, which means they have a non-negligible velocity dispersion that can affect structure formation on small scales.
    \end{itemize}

    Besides, there is also the \textbf{hidden corners} region (Green regions), it can break to 2 parts: the far right green region is the region of Macroscopic DM/ MACHOs (Massive Compact Halo Objects), which are astrophysical objects that can be made up of ordinary matter but are dark because they do not emit light (e.g. primordial black holes, brown dwarfs, neutron stars, etc.). They are usually massive ($> 1 M_\odot$). \\

    While the bottom green striped region is the region of Secluded Dark Sectors, it is like a "shadow universe" that contains its own particles and forces, they may have their own self-interactions and dark radiation, but they seldom "talk" to our standard model particles, make it hiding in the noise of the universe. As a results, these particles have a wide range of possible masses but only interact with ordinary matter by gravity.
\end{extra}

\begin{extra}
    From the above figure, we can see the theoretical fraction of dark matter that can be made up of PBHs(y-axis) against the mass of the PBHs in solar mass(x-axis). While The coloured regions represent the constraints from various observational methods: \\

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{(1) Microlensing} (Middle "dip" around $10^{-10} M_\odot$):
        \begin{itemize}
            \item intuition: Microlensing occurs when a massive compact object (like a PBH) passes in front of a distant star, causing a temporary increase in the star's brightness due to gravitational lensing. By monitoring the brightness of many stars, we can detect these microlensing events and constrain the abundance of PBHs in certain mass ranges.
            \item constraints: Microlensing surveys (e.g. MACHO, EROS, Kepler, HSC) have placed strong constraints on PBHs in the mass range of approximately $10^{-10} M_\odot$ to $10^{-2} M_\odot$, ruling out PBHs as the dominant component of dark matter in this mass range.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{(2) Femtolensing \& Evaporation} (Far left):
        \begin{itemize}
            \item intuition: tiny PBHs with masses less than about $10^{-15} M_\odot$ too small to lens stars, but they can still lens high-energy gamma rays from distant sources; while these tiny PBHs can also evaporate via Hawking radiation, producing gamma rays that can be detected by gamma-ray telescopes. If there were enough tiny PBHs, we would expect to see gamma-ray background(i.e. the sky glowing with gamma rays) from their evaporation.
            \item constraints: Femtolensing of gamma-ray bursts and the evaporation detected by Extragalactic Gamma-Ray Background (EGB) measurements can not observed interference patterns or excess gamma rays matching the expected signatures from PBHs, which places strong constraints on PBHs with masses less than about $10^{-15} M_\odot$.
        \end{itemize} 
        \item \textbf{(3) Dynamical Effects \& cosmic Heating}  (Far right):
        \begin{itemize}
            \item intuition: Heavy PBHs with masses greater than about $10^2 M_\odot$ can have significant gravitational effects on their surroundings:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Cosmic Heating: When heavy PBHs fly around the galaxy (e.g. Eridanus II/ Eri-II), their gravitational interactions can accrete surrounding gas and heat up the gas in the galaxy, which can be observed through X-ray emissions. 
                \item Early Universe Dynamics: If heavy PBHs were abundant in the early universe, their gravitational interactions could heat up the universe and distorting the CMB power spectrum, which can be observed through CMB measurements.
                \item Widely separated binary systems(WB): If heavy PBHs were abundant, they could disrupt widely separated binary systems (e.g. wider binaries in the Milky Way), which can be observed through stellar surveys.
            \end{itemize}
            \item constraints: 
                By looking for the absence of excess X-ray emissions from galaxies such as Eri-II, the lack of distortions in the CMB power spectrum, and the survival of widely separated binary systems, we can place strong constraints on PBHs with masses greater than about $10^2 M_\odot$, ruling out PBHs as the dominant component of dark matter in this mass range.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

    Meanwhile, the dashed arch in the middle (around $0.01$ to $1000 M_\odot$) represents the special constraint from the LIGO/Virgo gravitational wave direct detection of binary black hole mergers. If PBHs were abundant in this mass range, we would expect to see a significant number of mergers with masses corresponding to the PBH mass range. However, the observed merger rates and mass distributions from LIGO/Virgo do not match the expected signatures from PBHs, which places additional constraints on PBHs as dark matter candidates in this mass range (i.e. PBHs within this mass range ceiled by the dashed arch and can only make up a small fraction of the total dark matter density). \\

    Still, there is window at around $10^{-13} M_\odot$ to $10^{-14} M_\odot$ where PBHs can potentially make up significant fraction of dark matter $> 10\%$, which is not yet ruled out by current observations.
\end{extra}
